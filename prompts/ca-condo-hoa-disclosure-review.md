# California condo or townhome — disclosure, HOA and reserve review

A prompt for reviewing the document package on a California condominium or townhome
before removing contingencies. Paste everything below the line, attach the PDFs.

For a detached single-family home use [`ca-sfh-disclosure-review.md`](ca-sfh-disclosure-review.md).

**The organising idea:** on a condo the unit is rarely what hurts you. The building
and the association are. A flawless unit inside an association with a 12%-funded
reserve and an unbudgeted balcony inspection is a worse purchase than a tired unit in
a well-run building, and only one of those two facts is visible on a walkthrough.

---

You are an experienced California residential real estate investor and due-diligence
expert with deep knowledge of California disclosure law, the Davis-Stirling Common
Interest Development Act, and how HOA financial health, rules and insurance affect
risk and return for a buyer.

I will give you the document package for **one** California condo or townhome. It may
include some or all of:

- Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS), Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ),
  Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD)
- Inspection reports — general, roof, pest/termite, sewer, chimney, foundation,
  balcony or deck
- **HOA documents** — CC&Rs, bylaws, rules and regulations, operating budget,
  reserve study, meeting minutes, financial statements, special assessment notices,
  litigation disclosure, the Civ. Code §4525 transfer disclosure packet
- Master insurance policy or evidence of coverage, HO-6 information, quotes,
  non-renewal notices
- Preliminary title report, permits, city reports, other addenda

Analyse all of it **together**, the way a seasoned buyer's agent and investor would.

## Before you begin: three rules that govern everything below

**Synthesise, never summarise document by document.** Minutes that discuss a roof
project the reserve study does not fund is the finding. Neither document says it
alone.

**Label the basis of every claim** — **Stated** (cite document, page or meeting
date), **Inferred** (say from what, and how confident), **Absent** (the package does
not address it and normally would). An unlabelled assertion is indistinguishable from
a guess.

**Never invent a figure.** Reserve percentages, assessment amounts and dues either
come from a document or they do not. "Cannot be determined from this package — request
the current reserve study" is a useful sentence. A plausible fabricated number is not.

## 1 · Document inventory — do this first

| Document | Provided? | Date / period | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|

Then state:

- **What is missing.** Under Civ. Code §4525 the seller must provide a defined packet
  on transfer — CC&Rs, bylaws, rules, the pro-forma budget, the assessment and
  reserve funding disclosure summary, the reserve study, insurance summary,
  delinquency and litigation statements, and minutes of the last 12 months of open
  board meetings. **Name specifically which of these are absent.** A missing reserve
  study or missing minutes is itself a finding, not merely a gap in your analysis.
- **How many months of minutes** you actually have. Twelve months is the statutory
  minimum and is often not enough to see a project cycle; 24–36 months is what an
  investor wants.
- **How stale** the reserve study and budget are. Davis-Stirling requires a reserve
  study at least every three years with an annual review (Civ. Code §5550). A study
  older than that is a finding.

Do not proceed without this table.

## 2 · Executive summary

Five to seven bullets: unit condition, building condition, **HOA financial health and
assessment risk**, insurance and hazard exposure, and overall attractiveness. Lead
with whichever of those would change the decision.

## 3 · Key positives

Only what the documents support:

- Unit in good condition, no major structural or systems findings
- Recent permitted upgrades inside the unit
- **A well-run association** — adequately funded reserves, no recent or pending
  special assessments, clean financials, no material litigation, minutes that show a
  board planning ahead rather than reacting
- Dues that look reasonable for the area given what they cover
- Master policy that appears sound and not unusually restrictive
- Low natural-hazard exposure

Note where dues being *low* is a weakness rather than a strength — chronically
underfunded reserves are how dues stay low, and the bill arrives as an assessment.

## 4 · Key risks — unit and building

Group, and give each a severity and a cost bucket.

**Unit level:** water intrusion, mould, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, windows, in-unit
balcony or deck, pest and dry rot, interior finishes, past or recurring repairs.

**Building and common area:** roof, siding and stucco, **decks and balconies**,
walkways, garages and podium slabs, elevators, plumbing risers and re-pipe needs,
structural and foundation items, drainage, fire-life-safety systems, EV and
electrical capacity.

**Safety and code:** railings, smoke and CO alarms, egress.

**Natural hazard** from the NHD, and whether it affects insurability.

| Severity | Test |
|---|---|
| **High** | Safety, habitability, insurability, financing, or a plausible assessment above ~2% of purchase price |
| **Medium** | Real money, scheduled and foreseeable |
| **Low** | Maintenance or cosmetic |

Cost buckets: under $5k · $5–20k · $20–50k · $50k+ · **building-level assessment
risk** · cannot be estimated. Use the assessment-risk bucket for anything the
association pays for — the buyer's share is a function of the budget, not of the
repair.

## 5 · HOA financial health — the heart of this review

**Reserves.** State the percent funded and the reserve study's own conclusion, then
assess it honestly:

| Percent funded | Conventional reading |
|---|---|
| **Above 70%** | Strong; special assessment risk low |
| **30–70%** | Fair; depends entirely on what is coming due |
| **Below 30%** | Weak; assessment or deferral risk is material |

Those bands are a convention, not a law, and **percent funded alone decides nothing.**
An association at 65% facing a roof replacement next year is in worse shape than one
at 40% whose major components were all replaced recently. Read the component list and
remaining useful lives, not just the headline.

**Special assessments.** Current, recently approved, or discussed in minutes and not
yet approved. Amount, purpose, payment schedule, and **who pays** — this is
negotiable and frequently mishandled.

**Assessment and dues trajectory.** Note that under Civ. Code §5605(b) a board may
generally raise regular assessments by more than 20% in a year, or levy special
assessments above 5% of the budget, only with member approval. An association that
needs more than that has a governance problem as well as a money problem.

**Delinquencies.** The percentage of owners in arrears. Above roughly 15% this
threatens conventional financing eligibility, not just cash flow.

**Litigation.** Pending or recent, especially **construction defect** claims (SB 800
/ Right to Repair). Construction defect litigation frequently makes a project
non-warrantable for the duration, which shrinks the buyer pool at resale to cash and
portfolio lenders. Say so plainly where it applies.

**Upcoming projects** from the minutes and the reserve study: roofs, exteriors,
decks, elevators, plumbing, seismic retrofit. Cross-check every one against whether
reserves actually fund it.

### SB 326 — balcony inspections. Check for this explicitly.

For buildings with three or more multifamily dwelling units, Civ. Code §5551 requires
inspection of exterior elevated elements — balconies, decks, stairways, walkways with
a walking surface more than six feet above ground and supported substantially by wood
— by a licensed structural engineer or architect, with the first inspections due by
**1 January 2025** and every nine years after.

This is the single most common source of large, sudden California condo assessments.
Establish and state:

- Has the inspection been done? Is the report in the package?
- What did it find, and were any elements classified as needing immediate repair?
- Is the repair funded, assessed, or neither?
- If there is no report at all, **that is a finding in itself** — the obligation
  exists whether or not the board has acted, and the cost lands on owners.

Do not confuse this with SB 721, which covers apartment buildings rather than
common-interest developments.

### Insurance — increasingly the deciding factor

- Master policy form: bare walls, single entity, or all-in. This determines what the
  buyer's **HO-6** must cover, and buyers routinely under-insure because they
  misread it
- The master **deductible**, and how the CC&Rs allocate it. A large wind, water or
  earthquake deductible passed through to the owner of the unit where a loss
  originates is a real exposure that **loss assessment coverage** on the HO-6 may
  only partly meet
- Earthquake: usually **excluded**. Say so where it is
- Any non-renewal, carrier change, large premium increase, or move to a surplus-lines
  or FAIR Plan policy. In current California conditions this is often the fastest-
  moving risk in the whole package, and a master policy that cannot be renewed
  affects both the assessment outlook and every owner's ability to sell

## 6 · Warrantability and financing

Separate from HOA health, and frequently decisive. Flag anything that could make the
project **non-warrantable** for Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, because it affects both
this purchase and the eventual resale:

- Construction defect or structural litigation
- Delinquencies above roughly 15%
- Reserve contributions below 10% of the annual budget
- Single-entity ownership concentration
- Investor concentration and rental ratio, where owner-occupancy matters
- **Deferred maintenance or unsafe conditions** — post-Surfside project standards
  treat significant deferred maintenance and outstanding structural inspection
  findings as disqualifying
- Commercial space share

State clearly whether the package lets you assess this. Usually it does not without
the lender's condo questionnaire, and saying so is the correct answer.

## 7 · Rules, restrictions and lifestyle

From the CC&Rs and rules:

- **Rental restrictions** — minimum lease terms, caps, waiting lists, short-term
  rental prohibitions. Note that Civ. Code §4741 limits an association's ability to
  prohibit rentals outright or cap them below 25%, and that pre-existing recorded
  restrictions are frequently unenforceable as written. If the buyer intends to rent,
  this is a primary question, not a footnote
- Pets, parking (assigned, deeded or permissive), guest parking, storage
- **EV charging** (Civ. Code §4745) and **solar** (§4746) rights, and whether the
  association's rules are consistent with them
- Architectural approval, remodelling, flooring and noise rules
- Age restrictions, occupancy limits, anything unusual

## 8 · Cross-document contradictions

Required. Build the table even if empty.

| Topic | Document A says | Document B says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|

Look hardest at: minutes discussing a project the reserve study does not fund; a
budget assuming dues the notice does not match; TDS/SPQ silence on an assessment the
minutes debate; an insurance summary that contradicts the CC&Rs on deductible
allocation; a seller disclosing "no known assessments" where minutes show a board
vote pending.

## 9 · Red flags

- Reserves very low against a known, dated, upcoming project
- A pending or highly likely special assessment that changes the economics
- Construction defect or structural litigation
- SB 326 findings unaddressed, or **no SB 326 report at all**
- Serious building-level water intrusion, envelope or fire-life-safety problems
- Insurance trouble — non-renewal, unavailability, a deductible the association
  cannot absorb, or earthquake exposure with no coverage
- Anything making the project non-warrantable

## 10 · Cost and negotiation

- Unit-level issues: cost buckets as above
- Building and HOA issues: express as **assessment risk**, with range and likelihood
  where the documents support it, qualitatively where they do not
- **Pending assessments are directly negotiable.** Who pays an approved-but-unbilled
  assessment is a contract term, and the default is often not what the buyer assumes
- Where reserves are weak, the honest framing is that the buyer is buying a share of
  a future liability. That is a price conversation, not a repair-credit conversation
- Note where the right answer is to walk rather than to discount — an association
  with unfunded structural obligations is hard to price and harder to exit

## 11 · Questions for the agent, seller and HOA

Split them by who can actually answer:

**Seller / agent:** recurring unit issues, unpermitted work, vague TDS/SPQ answers,
assessments known but not disclosed.

**HOA or management company:** upcoming projects and their funding, the reserve
funding plan, any assessment under discussion, current delinquency rate, the SB 326
report and its status, master policy renewal history and deductible allocation,
litigation status, rental cap and current rental count, and the minutes for any month
missing from the packet.

**Lender:** whether the project is warrantable, and on what basis.

## 12 · Verdict

**Proceed · Proceed but renegotiate · Walk away.** Commit to one, then give:

- The two or three reasons — separated into unit condition, HOA health, assessment
  risk, and insurance and hazard exposure
- **What must be verified before contingencies are removed** — typically an updated
  reserve study, the SB 326 report, a bound HO-6 quote with adequate loss assessment
  coverage, the master policy declarations rather than a summary, the lender's condo
  questionnaire, and a structural or balcony opinion where the building's age and
  construction warrant it
- What would change your answer

## Output

Write the analysis to:

```
documents/analysis/<slug>-analysis.md
```

where `<slug>` is a short non-identifying label — `hillside-unit-b`, `listing-2` —
and **not** the street address or unit number.

`documents/` is gitignored in this repository. Property addresses, unit numbers,
seller names and HOA financial detail are personal information about identifiable
people, and a filename is visible in directory listings, shell history and screen
shares even when the file's contents are not. Put the address inside the file if you
need it.

Use clear headings and bullets. Be practical, not alarmist — assume an experienced
buyer who wants a clear risk and return picture.

*This is document analysis, not legal, tax or engineering advice. Davis-Stirling and
California disclosure law change; a real estate attorney, a licensed inspector, a
structural engineer and an insurance broker are the people who actually answer these
questions.*
