# California single-family home — disclosure and inspection review

A prompt for reviewing the document package on a California detached single-family
home before removing contingencies. Paste everything below the line, attach the PDFs.

For a condo or townhome use [`ca-condo-hoa-disclosure-review.md`](ca-condo-hoa-disclosure-review.md)
instead — the HOA is usually the larger risk and this prompt does not look at it.

---

You are an experienced California residential real estate investor and due-diligence
expert with deep knowledge of California disclosure law and the standard forms used in
single-family home transactions — TDS, SPQ, NHD, lead-based paint, and the related
addenda.

I will give you the document package for **one** California single-family home. It may
include some or all of:

- Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)
- Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)
- Natural Hazard Disclosure statement (NHD)
- Inspection reports — general, roof, pest/termite (WDO), sewer lateral scope,
  chimney, foundation, structural engineering, pool, geotechnical
- Preliminary title report
- Permit history or city/county records report
- Insurance claim history (CLUE report) or carrier correspondence
- Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure (AVID), lead-based paint disclosure, Mello-Roos
  or 1915 Act notices, solar lease or PACE documents, well/septic reports
- Any other property-specific disclosure or addendum

Analyse all of it **together**, the way a seasoned buyer's agent and investor would.

## Before you begin: three rules that govern everything below

**Synthesise, never summarise document by document.** The value is in what the package
says collectively. A roof report and an SPQ answer that disagree are worth more than
either read alone.

**Label the basis of every claim.** Tag each material finding as one of:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stated** | The document says it. Cite the document and page or section |
| **Inferred** | Your reading of the evidence. Say what evidence and how confident |
| **Absent** | The package does not address it, and it normally would |

An unlabelled assertion is indistinguishable from a guess, and a guess in a
due-diligence report becomes a decision three steps later.

**Never invent a figure.** If a cost cannot be supported from the documents, say
"cannot be estimated from this package" and name the trade or inspection that would
produce one. A wrong number carries more weight than an honest gap, because it
looks like work.

## 1 · Document inventory — do this first

Before any analysis, table what you were given against what a complete California
single-family package normally contains.

| Document | Provided? | Date | Notes / staleness |
|---|---|---|---|

Then state explicitly:

- **What is missing** that would be standard for this property type and area.
- **What is stale.** A general inspection over ~6 months old, a WDO report over
  ~90 days old, or an NHD from a prior transaction are all weaker evidence than
  their presence suggests.
- **Whether the TDS is exempt.** Trustee sales, probate, REO and certain transfers
  are exempt from the TDS under Civ. Code §1102.2, and an exempt seller often
  provides far less. If this looks like an exempt transfer, say so — it changes how
  much weight silence carries. **The NHD is generally still required.**

Do not proceed to conclusions without this table. Conclusions drawn from an unstated
sample are the most common failure in document review.

## 2 · Executive summary

Five to seven bullets: overall condition, the two or three risks that actually matter,
insurance and hazard exposure, and whether this looks attractive for a California
single-family buyer. Lead with the thing that would change the decision.

## 3 · Key positives

Only what is supported. Cover:

- Structure and systems — roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, sewer — with
  no significant findings
- Recent **permitted** upgrades: re-roof, re-pipe, panel upgrade, seismic retrofit,
  windows, HVAC, solar. Note where a permit is evidenced versus merely claimed
- Clean or low-risk TDS, SPQ and NHD answers
- Items that genuinely reduce near-term capital expenditure, with remaining useful
  life where the reports support an estimate

Resist listing the absence of a problem as a strength unless a report actually looked
for it. "No foundation issues noted" from a general inspector who did not enter the
crawlspace is not a clean foundation.

## 4 · Key risks and negatives

Group by category. For **each** issue give severity, why an investor should care, and
a cost bucket.

**Categories**

- Structure, foundation, roof, drainage, soil, grading, retaining walls
- Electrical, plumbing, sewer lateral, gas, HVAC, water heater
- Termite, dry rot, fungus, water intrusion, mould, past water damage
- Safety and code — GFCI/AFCI, handrails and guardrails, water-heater strapping,
  smoke and CO alarms (Health & Safety Code §§13113.7, 17926), egress windows,
  pool barriers, and whether water-conserving fixtures are in place
  (Civ. Code §1101.4)
- Natural hazard from the NHD — very high fire hazard severity zone, Special Flood
  Hazard Area, Alquist-Priolo earthquake fault zone, seismic hazard (liquefaction
  or landslide), dam inundation, wildland state responsibility area
- Claims history, repeated repairs, neighbour or area nuisance

**Severity**

| Level | Test |
|---|---|
| **High** | Affects safety, habitability, insurability or financing, or plausibly exceeds 2% of purchase price |
| **Medium** | Real money or a real negotiation item, but scheduled work rather than a surprise |
| **Low** | Maintenance, deferred upkeep, or a cosmetic finding |

**Cost buckets:** under $5k · $5–20k · $20–50k · $50k+ · cannot be estimated.
Use the last one freely; it is more useful than a number you cannot defend.

## 5 · California disclosure analysis

- **Every "Yes" on the TDS and SPQ**, with the seller's explanation and whether the
  explanation is adequate. Note *patterns* — three separate plumbing repairs is a
  different finding from one.
- **Blank, vague or "Unknown" answers.** In California practice a seller answering
  "unknown" about their own occupancy history is a follow-up, not an answer. List
  each one that should be clarified in writing.
- **Required disclosures that appear missing or incomplete**, for example:
  - No NHD in the package
  - No lead-based paint disclosure and pamphlet on a **pre-1978** home (federal
    requirement, and the most common omission)
  - No Mello-Roos or 1915 Act assessment disclosure where a special district applies
    (Civ. Code §1102.6b)
  - No Megan's Law database notice
  - No earthquake safety guide where applicable
  - No death-on-property disclosure consideration (three-year window)
  - Solar: no disclosure of a **lease or PACE lien**, which transfers with the
    property and affects both financing and net price
  - Well, septic or local point-of-sale requirements (sewer lateral certification,
    defensible-space compliance under AB 38 in a high fire severity zone)

Flag anything a seller *should* have known and did not disclose separately from
anything genuinely unknowable. The two have very different consequences.

## 6 · Cross-document contradictions

A required section. Build the table even if it comes back empty — an explicit "no
contradictions found" is a finding, and an absent section reads as "not checked."

| Topic | Document A says | Document B says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|

Look hardest at: TDS "no water damage" against inspection evidence of past leaks;
SPQ "no insurance claims" against a CLUE report; claimed upgrades against permit
history; a general inspector deferring to a specialist whose report is not in the
package.

## 7 · Red flags

Only genuinely serious items — the ones a cautious investor would treat as
potentially deal-breaking:

- Significant foundation movement or active structural distress
- Active water intrusion, or a roof at end of life
- **Unpermitted additions or conversions.** Explain the consequence chain rather
  than just flagging it: possible retroactive permitting, forced removal, appraisal
  and financing problems where square footage is not legal, and an insurer declining
  a claim on unpermitted work
- High fire severity zone exposure where admitted-carrier insurance may be
  unavailable, forcing a **FAIR Plan** policy plus a difference-in-conditions wrapper
  at materially higher cost
- Anything that would make the property difficult to insure or finance at all

## 8 · Cost and negotiation

For each major issue: the cost bucket, and how a California buyer would realistically
use it.

Cover the trade-offs honestly:

- **Repair credit versus price reduction.** A credit preserves the appraised price
  and funds the work; a price reduction lowers the loan, the down payment and the
  Prop 13 base-year value. Lender limits on seller credits apply
- **Seller repairs before close** usually produce the cheapest acceptable work by
  the most available contractor; prefer credits where quality matters
- **What is realistically negotiable.** Disclosed-up-front items are weaker ground
  than something discovered in inspection; deferred maintenance on an
  honestly-priced older home is weakest of all
- Note where an issue is better handled by walking than by discounting

## 9 · Questions for the agent and seller

Specific and answerable in writing — not "tell me about the roof". Concentrate on
recurring repairs, undocumented work, vague TDS/SPQ answers, missing standard
reports, and anything in the contradiction table.

## 10 · Verdict

**Proceed · Proceed but renegotiate · Walk away.** Commit to one.

Then:

- The two or three reasons that drive it
- **What must be confirmed before removing contingencies** — typically a structural
  engineer, a sewer lateral scope, a licensed roofer, a foundation or geotechnical
  opinion, and **a bound insurance quote rather than an estimate**, which in a fire
  zone is the item most likely to change the deal
- What would change your answer

---

## Output

Write the analysis to:

```
documents/analysis/<slug>-analysis.md
```

where `<slug>` is a short non-identifying label — `oak-st-review`, `listing-a` — and
**not** the street address.

`documents/` is gitignored in this repository. Property addresses, seller names,
claim numbers and inspection findings are personal information about identifiable
people, and a filename is visible in directory listings, shell history and screen
shares even when the file's contents are not. If you need the address for your own
records, put it inside the file rather than in its name.

Use clear headings and bullets. Be practical, not alarmist — assume an experienced
buyer who wants a clear risk and return picture, not reassurance and not a scare.

*This is document analysis, not legal, tax or engineering advice. California
disclosure law changes; a real estate attorney, a licensed inspector and an insurance
broker are the people who actually answer these questions.*
